    Electric field integrals

    🔗

    5.

    In the following exercise, steps 1-11 in a solution to an electric field integral problem are given. Afterwards, there are reflection questions for your team to answer based on carefully reading the solution and the commentary about the reasoning. You should begin by lightly skimming the problem and solution, then when you get to the questions, go back over the solution more slowly and carefully to answer the reflection questions.
    🔗
    Example problem:
    🔗
    Find the electric field vector at point \(P\text{,}\) which is given a perpendicular distance \(r\) above an infinitely long, thin wire of given electric change density \(\lambda\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Example solution:
    🔗
    Steps 1 through 2:
    🔗
    Since electric field strength depends on the amount of charge and the sensor’s distance from the charge, steps 1 through 4 are about finding the charge and distance of just one charge element.
    🔗
    Slice the parent object into "charge elements, all of same shape. Only shapes whose electric field formulae are already known in our formula library may be used as charge elements. Slicing should advance along a coordinate axis (whether Cartesian or polar). Each charge element is on infinitesimal distance further along the coordinate axis.
    🔗
    Shade in one generally representative slice (not in a unique spot like 0 centimeters, 4 centimeters, or \(L\) centimeters). We’ll use the generaized coordinates and generalized size of this charge element to write a general formala good for the electric field emanating from any one of the ofher charge elements.
    🔗
    Step 3:
    🔗
    Since the parent object is a 1-D wire, So is the charge element. A 1-D object’s size is its length.
    \begin{align*} \text{size of slice} \amp = \text{arclength} \\ \amp = dx \end{align*}
    We call the slice’s charge \(dQ\) since \(Q\) is the charge of the whole parent object. (Alternatively, you could say \(Q_{slice}\) instead of \(dQ\text{.}\))
    \begin{align*} \text{charge of slice} \amp = dQ \\ \amp = \text{density} \cdot \text{size of slice} \\ dQ \amp = \lambda \cdot dx \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 4:
    🔗
    We need a formula that gives the distance from the generalized coordinates of the centroid of the charge element to the field point.
    \begin{align*} \text{distance from slice to sensor} \amp \equiv d \\ d \amp = \sqrt{x^{2} + r^{2}} \end{align*}
    The \(dQ\) and \(d\) formulae you just made are unique to this parent object, and would even have been different if you had chosen a different slicing method/axìs.
    🔗
    Steps 5-6 are about using the charge and distance formulae from steps 3-4 to compute the electric field emanating from just one charge element.
    🔗
    Step 5:
    🔗
    Draw the direction of the electric field emanating from the charge element. Define the angle of that direction. If the angle is diagonal, make sure you could fully express the angle using previously defined variables.
    🔗
    Put the electric field emanating from the charge element in its own free-body diagram. We call the slice’s electric Field \(dE\) since \(E\) is the electric field of the whole parent object. (Alternatively, you could say \(E_{slice}\) instead of \(dE\text{.}\)) Resolve the \(dE\) vector into x and y components (if \(dE\) is diagonal trig. is needed).
    \begin{equation*} dE_{x} = - \lvert dE \rvert \sin \theta \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} dE_{y} = \lvert dE \rvert \cos \theta. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 6: Determine \(dE\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} dE = E_{point} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} dE = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{q_{source}}{r^{2}} \end{equation*}
    The one slice is the source. Note \(dE\) means the electric field of this slice, So We must calculate it out using this one slice’s formulae. Look in your electric field formula library for the gereric formula corresponding to the shape of the charge element (not the parent object). (In this problem, we need \(E_{point}\) since the charge elemerts are infinitesìmally short 1-D objects.) In the library this generic \(r\) meant the source to sensor distance (not the same \(r\) as in this problem)!
    \begin{equation*} dE = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{Q_{slice}}{{distance}^{2}} \end{equation*}
    Note that \(q_{source} = Q_{slice} = dQ\) and \(r = \text{distance from the slice}.\)
    \begin{equation*} dE = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{dQ}{{distance}^{2}} \end{equation*}
    Note that the charge of the slice is \(dQ = \lambda dx\) and \(\text{distance} = \sqrt{x^{2} + r^{2}}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} dE = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{\left( \lambda dx \right)}{\left( \sqrt{x^{2} + r^{2}} \right)^{2}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now we substitute in the expanded formula for \(dE\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} dE_{x} = -\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{ \lambda dx }{\sqrt{x^{2} + r^{2}}^{2}} \sin \theta \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} dE_{y} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{ \lambda dx }{\sqrt{x^{2} + r^{2}}^{2}} \cos \theta \end{equation*}
    Steps 7-11 are about summing up the electric fields of each single slice to get the grand total electric field of the parent object. We do this summation by setting up an integral.
    \begin{equation*} dE_{x} = -\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{ \lambda dx }{x^{2} + r^{2}} \sin \theta \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} dE_{y} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{ \lambda dx }{x^{2} + r^{2}} \cos \theta \end{equation*}
    We’d like to integrate \(dE_{x}\) and \(dE_{y}\text{,}\) but they have two non-independent variable (\(\theta\) is a function of \(x\)). We can’t integrate until it’s a single variable. \(\frac{dx}{x^{2} + r^{2}}\) is easiest to integrate by writing \(x\) and \(dx\) in terms of \(\theta\text{.}\) This is called "trig. sub." The trig. sub, reference triangle is already in your diagram! Other problems will have a different \(dE\) which may not need a trig. sub.
    🔗
    Step 7:
    🔗
    Write all x’s in terms of \(\theta\) ("trig. sub.")
    \begin{align*} \tan \theta \amp = \frac{x}{r} \\ x \amp = r \tan \theta \\ dx \amp = d \left( r \tan \theta \right) \\ dx \amp = r d \left( \tan \theta \right) \\ dx \amp = r \sec^{2} \theta d\theta \end{align*}
    Furthermore,
    \begin{align*} \amp x^{2} + r^{2} \\ \amp = \left( r \tan \theta \right)^{2} + r^{2} \\ \amp = r^{2} \left(\tan \theta + 1 \right)^{2} \\ \amp = r^{2} \sec^{2} \theta. \end{align*}
    Then
    \begin{equation*} dE_{x} = -\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{ \lambda \left( r \sec^{2} \theta d\theta \right) }{\left( r^{2} \sec^{2} \theta \right)} \sin \theta \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} dE_{x} = -\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{ \lambda d\theta }{r} \sin \theta \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} dE_{y} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{ \lambda \left( r \sec^{2} \theta d\theta \right) }{\left( r^{2} \sec^{2} \theta \right)} \cos \theta \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} dE_{y} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0}} \frac{ \lambda d\theta }{r} \cos \theta. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 8:
    🔗
    Total \(E_{x} = \text{sum of contributions from all slices}\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} E_{x} = \int dE_{x} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} E_{y} = \int dE_{y}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Step 9:
    🔗
    We need limits of integration. The limits specity the coordinates where the object starts and ends (first slice’s and last slice’s coords).
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int dE_{x} = \int_{x = -\infty}^{x = +\infty} -\frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon r} \sin \theta d\theta \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \int dE_{y} = \int_{x = -\infty}^{x = +\infty} \frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon r} \cos \theta d\theta \end{equation*}
    The x-limits are from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\text{,}\) but the integration variable is \(\theta\text{.}\) So we need to compute the angle when \(x \to -\infty\) and when \(x \to +\infty\text{.}\) For the angle when \(x \to -\infty\) we have
    \begin{align*} \tan \theta_{i} \amp = \frac{x_{i}}{r} \\ \theta_{i} \amp = \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{x_{i}}{r} \right) \\ \theta_{i} \amp = \tan^{-1}\left( -\infty \right) \\ \theta_{i} \amp = \frac{-\pi}{2}. \end{align*}
    For the angle when \(x \to +\infty\) we have
    \begin{align*} \tan \theta_{f} \amp = \frac{x_{f}}{r} \\ \theta_{f} \amp = \tan^{-1}\left( \frac{x_{f}}{r} \right) \\ \theta_{f} \amp = \tan^{-1}\left( \infty \right) \\ \theta_{f} \amp = \frac{\pi}{2}. \end{align*}
    Now we apply limits and finish the summations via integration. Then we have
    \begin{align*} \int dE_{x} \amp = \int_{\theta = \frac{-\pi}{2}}^{\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}} -\frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \sin \theta d\theta \\ \\ \amp = -\frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \int_{\theta = \frac{-\pi}{2}}^{\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}} \sin \theta d\theta \\ \\ \amp = -\frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \left( -\cos \theta \right) \Bigg|_{\frac{-\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \\ \\ \amp = -\frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \left( -\cos \frac{\pi}{2} - \left( - \cos -\frac{\pi}{2} \right) \right) \\ \\ \amp = -\frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \left( -0 - \left( -0 \right) \right) \\ \\ \int dE_{x} \amp = 0 \end{align*}
    and
    \begin{align*} \int dE_{y} \amp = \int_{\theta = \frac{-\pi}{2}}^{\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \cos \theta d\theta \\ \\ \amp = \frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \int_{\theta = \frac{-\pi}{2}}^{\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}} \cos \theta d\theta \\ \\ \amp = \frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \left( \sin \theta \right) \Bigg|_{\frac{-\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \\ \\ \amp = \frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \left( \sin \frac{\pi}{2} - \sin -\frac{\pi}{2} \right) \\ \\ \amp = \frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \left( 1 - \left( -1 \right) \right) \\ \\ \amp = \frac{\lambda}{4 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \left( 2 \right) \\ \\ \int dE_{y} \amp = \frac{\lambda}{2 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Step 10:
    🔗
    Check whether plus or minus signs on \(E_{x}\text{,}\) \(E_{y}\) make sense for direction parent object would emanate the electric field. Fix if needed.
    🔗
    Step 11:
    🔗
    If requested, format answer as a vector.
    \begin{equation*} \vec{E}_{\text{infinite line}} = \langle 0, \frac{\lambda}{2 \pi \varepsilon_{0} r} \rangle \end{equation*}
